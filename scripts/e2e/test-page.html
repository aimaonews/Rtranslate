The Quiet Power of Slow Technology

In an industry obsessed with speed, a small movement of developers and designers is rediscovering the value of doing less, more deliberately. They call it slow technology: software that respects attention instead of competing for it.

The principles are simple. Notifications arrive in batches instead of interrupting every few minutes. Features are removed until only the essential remains. Interfaces default to calm colors and generous whitespace.

Consider the example of a note-taking application built by a two-person team in Oslo. It has no social features, no collaboration, no artificial intelligence. It stores plain text files and syncs them between devices. Yet it has attracted more than two hundred thousand paying users who describe it as a relief.

Developers explain that the tool respects a basic truth: attention is the scarcest resource of modern life. Every pixel that does not serve the reader's goal is a small tax on their concentration.

Practical guidelines

  • Measure success in tasks completed, not minutes spent in the app.
  • Prefer plain text and open formats over proprietary lock-in.
  • Batch network requests to reduce mental fragmentation and battery drain.

The movement is not against progress. It argues that true progress sometimes looks like subtraction. As one engineer put it, "the best interface is the one you forget you are using."
